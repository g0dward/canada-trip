Three railways and a chocolate factory

You are staying inside a triangle drawn by rail lines on all three sides. That is not a metaphor. It is why the neighbourhood had no name for a century, why it filled with factories, why it emptied, and why it smells of Kit Kats.

Distance
4.4 km, about 50 minutes of walking, two to three hours with stops. Starts and ends at your own door.
Best day
Sunday 16 August, starting around 09:15. MOCA is the last stop and 16 August is its final day before it shuts until October. It opens at 10:00. Walk the tour, arrive at the doors as they open, and the rest of that Sunday is still free for Geary Avenue at 15:00.
After the 16th
The walk still works. You just finish at the outside of a closed gallery, which is a slightly bleaker ending than the one intended.
Watch for
The Railpath may be closed on the stretch you need. There is a street-level fallback below and the walk survives either way.

The argument

Built by rails, emptied by the same logic that built it, resold as culture. And one factory that never left.

Everything here arrived because of the tracks. The Gurney Foundry, the Comfort Soap Works, the Campbell Flour Mill, Standard Foundry, Nordheimer Piano, Canadian Cycle. Note the verb though: these firms did not grow here, they moved here. This was Toronto's first industrial suburb, factories quitting the crowded core for cheap land with a railway siding attached.

Which is the joke, and the tour's spine. The exact logic that built this place is the logic that later took the jobs to Mississauga and hollowed it out. The neighbourhood was a beneficiary of industrial decentralisation and then a casualty of it, for the same reason, about eighty years apart.

The name is the giveaway. It did not have one. A local project called Fuzzy Boundaries ran a public vote in March 2010, more than two hundred suggestions came in, and "Junction Triangle" won. Your neighbourhood is sixteen years old and the buildings in it are a hundred and twenty.

The walk

Out the door, south, west across the tracks, then south along the corridor and back up a dead end street to the gallery.

Stop one

Lansdowne and Wallace

Six minutes from the doorMap

Start with the lofts. These are the converted works of the firms above, and the City's own heritage survey makes the pattern explicit: they were "typically constructed adjacent to railway lines." Not near. Adjacent. The siding was the point.

Worth knowing what else was made within a few hundred metres of here before 1910: soap, flour, pianos, bicycles and cars. Canadian Cycle became C.C.M., and produced the Russell Model A up at Weston Road and St Clair. There was a car industry on this ground before the First World War.

Halo Brewery is on the corner at 247 Wallace if it is open, which is genuinely uncertain in 2026, so treat it as a maybe rather than a stop.

Stop two

The Wallace Avenue footbridge

Wallace at Sousa Mendes, by number 391Map
540 m from stop one7 min

The best object on the walk, and the whole tour compressed into one piece of steel.

Built 1907 for four and a half thousand dollars. It was the Ontario Bridge Company's very first design. It is a Warren pony truss, which means the equilateral steel triangles along the sides are doing the structural work, and it is one of very few left in the city. It crosses the tracks at an oblique angle rather than a right angle, which almost nothing else does.

And it was meant to be temporary. Heritage Toronto puts it plainly:

Once the Wallace Avenue Pedestrian Bridge was built however, people liked it and the bridge became a permanent feature of the Junction Triangle neighbourhood.

Heritage Toronto

Before it existed, getting to a shop, a school or a job on the other side of the tracks meant walking north to Dupont or south to Bloor. Stand on it and notice that this is still the walking problem here, and that a stopgap from 1907 is still solving it. Every industry it was built to serve has gone. The bridge is protected under the Ontario Heritage Act.

Stop three

The West Toronto Railpath

Directly under the bridge, heading southMap
1.6 km along the path22 min

Drop down onto the path and walk south. It runs on former rail land right along the corridor, and the geography is unusually tidy: its southern end is the mouth of Sterling Road, so the path delivers you to the next three stops without a single road crossing.

The argument to carry down it: the same corridor that cut this neighbourhood into pieces has been converted, one narrow strip at a time, into the thing that stitches it back together. Hold the applause though. The southern extension, roughly two more kilometres with four new bridges, has been promised for years, had still not started as of January 2026, and the budget is approaching two hundred million dollars. Locally it has become a joke.

This is the one live risk on the walk. The Railpath reopened after a four-year closure, but an August 2026 local source says another closure is expected "from Perth to Dundas Street later this year", which is exactly the stretch you want. If it is shut: from the footbridge take Sousa Mendes Street down the west side of the corridor (that street exists because of the corridor), out to Perth Avenue, south to Bloor, then east to Sterling Road and south. Slightly longer, all pavement, and it has the advantage of putting you on Perth, which is the street the triangle actually lived on.
Stop four

Nestlé

72 Sterling RoadMap
230 m north from the path's end3 min

Here is the factory that never left, and the reason this walk is not simply another elegy for industry.

Sterling Road was cut through a lumber merchant's land by 1903, between the Canadian Pacific and the Grand Trunk. A chocolate factory opened at number 72 that same year: Cowan's. Rowntree's bought it in the 1920s and brought its own line with it. Aero, 1935. Smarties, 1938. Kit Kat later. Nestlé runs it now and still makes the Rowntree's inheritance: Kit Kat, Coffee Crisp, Aero, Smarties, twenty-four hours a day, three hundred and sixty-five days a year, with several hundred people inside.

Everything else on this street has been converted, renamed, or turned into an art gallery. Number 72 is still making confectionery and you can smell it from the pavement. That is the stop. Stand there and breathe in.

There is no public tour and there is nothing to go into. Nestlé has run occasional invite-only visits, and it publishes a visitor guide for suppliers and staff, but nothing you can book. Also worth one line as proof this is a real working plant rather than a heritage prop: production stopped during a strike in 2024 and restarted after a three-year deal.
Stop five

The pickle works

128 Sterling Road, now Henderson BrewingMap
350 m5 min

Built 1908 for T.A. Lytle and Company: pickles and vinegar. Lytle himself was an Irish immigrant who arrived in 1871 and died in 1911 at sixty-six. The operation wound up in the 1920s and the building went to Scythes and Co., which became Flying Colours International in 2001 and moved out in 2014.

Now it is Henderson Brewing, and this is where the two Junction walks shake hands. A works that fermented things for a living, sitting a few hundred metres outside a district that had outlawed drinking. In 2017 Henderson released a limited edition beer called The Ghost of Prohibition. If you want to know what that is about, do the other walk.

Do not go looking for the Drake Commissary. It closed to the public in August 2021 and Heritage Toronto's page still lists it, which tells you how old that page is. Henderson's own listed hours are from ten in the morning, but they are listing-sourced, so check on the day.
Stop six

MOCA, in the aluminium works

158 Sterling RoadMap
155 m2 min

Built 1919 to 1920 for the Northern Aluminum Company, later Alcan. It made cooking utensils and supplies for Canadian forces in the First World War, and by the Second the company held roughly three quarters of aluminium production across the British Empire. It stayed here more than fifty years, turning out kitchen tools, bottle caps and car parts. Tower Automotive took it on after 2000, went bankrupt in 2006, and left its name on the building.

Now the detail that changes how you look at the galleries. The architect was John W. Woodman, working with C.A.P. Turner, the American engineer who commercialised the flat concrete slab carried on mushroom columns. So the flared capitals you can see inside are not a happy accident of reuse and not decoration. They are the reason the building could be built this tall, designed with the man whose technique they are. ERA Architects, who did the heritage work in the 2015 conversion, specifically prioritised keeping them visible. The front doors are the factory's own wooden bi-fold loading doors, refurbished rather than replaced.

16 August is the last day of the season. Kimsooja on floors one and three, Sara Cwynar on two, Thenjiwe Niki Nkosi in the south stairwell. Then it shuts until 3 October. Sunday hours are 10:00 to 17:00, adult $14, and floor one is always free.

One honest note to end on, because this walk should not become a redevelopment brochure. Heritage Toronto's own account of the area's art-led revival records that as early as January 2016, before MOCA even opened, some local art studios saw rents rise by over 55 per cent, and that while people now come here for the art and culture, many of the artists and cultural workers are gradually being driven out. The gallery in the factory is a good building doing good work. It is also the instrument of something, and the people it displaced were the previous tenants of exactly the same cheap industrial space.

If you want more of it

Two extensions, both optional, neither needed.

North up the Railpath, 3.2 km round. From the footbridge, north to the path's top end at Cariboo Avenue, then east to the Galleria site at Dupont and Dufferin: twenty acres of half-demolished shopping mall becoming eight buildings, 2,900 condominiums, 150 affordable rentals and an eight-acre park, master plan by Hariri Pontarini. Expect hoardings and cranes. It is the same story as Sterling Road told in the present tense, with the outcome not yet known.

Geary Avenue is a separate evening. It is 600 m east of the Galleria site and it is a drinking street now, so it reads better after dark and it is already on the main list. Do not bolt it onto this.
