Their ways are not ours

Toronto brags about its Chinatown, its Kensington, its Little Italy, its Little Jamaica. This walk starts on the neighbourhood they all came from, which the city demolished and built its City Hall on. The ethnic neighbourhoods are not the story. They are the diaspora of the story.

Distance
About 4.6 km of actual walking, flat. Budget four to five hours on the ground including lunch, six door to door.
Getting there
Lansdowne, Line 2 east to Bloor-Yonge, Line 1 south two stops to Dundas, surface at the Eaton Centre. Honestly 30 to 40 minutes.
Which day
Any weekday. Friday has two bonuses: Old City Hall sometimes opens free on Fridays, and the Chinatown food tour runs. Check both below.
Heat plan
Start by 10:00. The Ward blocks are exposed concrete; Kensington has awnings and shade. Do the exposed half first and land in the Market for the hot early afternoon. Buy water early, the Ward stretch has few shops.
Ends at
A cash-friendly dive patio in Kensington, with an optional dinner extension that completes the argument.

Before you go: the tours that already exist

You asked whether anyone already runs this. The answer is yes, commercially, and the two best institutional ones miss your week by days on either side.

Bookable in your window

Tour Guys, "Cool Toronto: Kensington Market & Chinatown." Their own blurb is this walk's thesis in a sentence: "walk through waves of immigration, community activism, and stubborn creativity." 2.5 hours, maximum 8 people, about $69 with a coffee and a snack included, meets beside the AGO at McCaul and Dundas. Book at tourguys.ca at least 24 hours ahead; if nobody books they do not send a guide, and the listing historically books out well in advance.

Culinary Adventure Co, Chinatown + Kensington food tour. Friday to Sunday only at 11:00, so in your week that means Friday 21 August. $129, three hours, 18-plus tastings, meets under the Cat on the Chair sculpture at Spadina and St Andrew. Verify the price when booking, their page is undated.

The connoisseur's option: Shirley Lum invented this genre. She has run multicultural Kensington and Chinatown walks since July 1993 and now does mostly private customised tours (torontowalksbikes.com). A direct booking with her would beat everything above. Unverified whether she has August availability; worth one email.

Near misses, and the free consolations

Heritage Toronto's "Changing Chinatown" ran 12 August, five days before your window, and repeats 12 September. Their "Big Stories in Little India" runs Saturday 22 August at 3pm, the day you drive north. But that one exists as a free self-guided map tour on heritagetoronto.org, usable any day, and it is how this page tells the Little India story below without sending you there.

The Museum of Toronto has no walks this week but two on-theme exhibitions: "You Belong Here" at the Miles Nadal JCC, 750 Spadina at Bloor, an exhibition about belonging housed in the Jewish community centre that marks how far north the Kensington Jewish community travelled; and "The T.O. You Don't Know" at 401 Richmond, itself a rehabilitated garment factory. Hours unverified for both, check museumoftoronto.com.

The Friday wildcard: Old City Hall. The building that policed the Ward ran free Friday openings with a Ward Museum migration exhibition in summer 2025, and the City says it still opens "occasionally" for pop-ups. Check toronto.ca/OldCityHall the week of the walk. If it is open on Friday the 21st, walking into it is the best indoor stop available, and free.

The argument

One neighbourhood, erased in stages. Everything the city now celebrates is where its people went.

St. John's Ward, always just The Ward, ran from Queen up to College between Yonge and University. From the 1840s it was where you landed: Catholic Irish, then Black families who had come north on the Underground Railroad, then from the 1890s Eastern European Jews, Italians and Chinese, as Toronto ballooned from 56,000 people in 1871 to 376,000 in 1911. It held Toronto's first Chinatown, its first Little Italy, its Jewish garment quarter, and a Black church that stood for over a century.

John Lorinc, who edited the two books this walk leans on, states the problem you are about to have plainly:

To find The Ward today, in fact, we must imagine our way back into a complicated world whose physical traces have been systematically expunged.

John Lorinc, The Ward, 2015

The report that killed it wore a lab coat

In July 1911, Medical Officer of Health Dr. Charles Hastings published his investigation of "slum conditions". His inspectors had visited 4,693 houses; in the Ward they found 11,645 people in 1,653 homes, 139 families living in one room, 61 families in basements, and one in a cellar. His words, verbatim:

The public is awake to the necessity of prompt action, and is cognizant of the fact that the best way to beautify a city is to 'clean it up.'

Their ways are not ours. Every effort must be made to familiarize our new citizens with our sanitary standards.

Dr. Charles Hastings, 1911

Two honest complications. Hastings also attacked the slumlords, demanding to know why anyone should be permitted to charge a foreigner "who is entirely at his mercy" rent out of all proportion to the property, and he called for housing bylaws and garden suburbs. And his report ordered no demolition. What it did was fix the word slum to the district in the official record. The word did the demolition work forty years later.

The erasure came in stages: Toronto General took the block south of College in 1913, and the University Avenue hospitals ate westward for decades. Then the coup de grace: in 1947 voters approved a new civic square, nobody consulted the people in its footprint, and through the 1950s the City expropriated Elizabeth Street. By 1955 the expropriations had taken the livelihoods of nearly 500 people in Chinatown alone; by 1965 two thirds of Chinatown was gone and Viljo Revell's New City Hall stood on it. Arlene Chan, the historian of Toronto's Chinatowns, is blunt about the compensation: "They didn't get fair market value for the land."

In 1967 the city's development commissioner proposed demolishing the rest, arguing that preserving Chinatown would create a "ghetto". What stopped him was a restaurateur named Jean Lumb, who founded the Save Chinatown Committee and won at council in 1969. Her argument to the Toronto Star deserves to be read on the spot where her restaurant stood:

We should have a spot for them to start from, a place where they can be among their own people, hear their own language spoken… Some people say a Chinatown encourages ghettos and this is a reason why it shouldn't be, but that's not so. It just gives the people a sense of belonging. It's a nice environment for them until they're ready to go on their way more and fit into the Canadian community.

Jean Lumb, Toronto Star, 1967

She had already, in 1957, been the only woman in the delegation that pressed Prime Minister Diefenbaker to strip the racial discrimination that survived in immigration law. In 1976 she became the first Chinese Canadian woman appointed to the Order of Canada. Her plaque stands in the erased Chinatown; her laneway in the one that survived. The walk visits both.

The walk

Stand on the erasure first. Then walk west to the places the erased people went.

Stop one

Church of the Holy Trinity

19 Trinity Square, behind the Eaton CentreMap

Start with the stop built for a British visitor. This 1847 church was funded by an anonymous £5,000 bequest from England, on one condition: that the pews be "free and unappropriated forever", at a time when churches charged pew rents that priced the poor out of God. Fifty years passed before the donor was identified: Mary Lambert Swale of Settle, Yorkshire, who died in May 1845 aged 25 and never saw Canada.

Bishop Strachan announced it as the "Parochial Church of the Poor of Toronto", and it became the Ward's conscience, one of its three founding institutions alongside the Black church and the Poor House. It still runs on that politics today, surrounded on three sides by a shopping mall that was built around it because it refused to sell.

Stop two

Old City Hall

60 Queen St WMap
200 m3 min

Not a remnant of the Ward but the building that loomed over it. E. J. Lennox's 1899 courthouse-and-hall processed the Ward's bootleggers and unlicensed pedlars through its police court, and its clock tower stared straight down Elizabeth Street, the Ward's spine. The courts moved out in 2023 and the building sits empty, awaiting its next use. If it happens to be open (the Friday wildcard above), go in.

Stop three

Nathan Phillips Square

100 Queen St WMap
150 m2 min

You are now standing on First Chinatown. The square, the arches, the skating rink, the two curved towers: all of it sits on expropriated Elizabeth Street. This is what the Ward was erased for, and it is genuinely good modernism, which is the uncomfortable part. The city got a great civic square and the people who lived here got below-market compensation and dispersal.

Somewhere along the square's west edge is a plaque commemorating the uprooting. Note who paid for it: not the City, but community fundraising led by Kristyn Wong-Tam, then head of the Chinese business association. The city built the square; the community had to buy the apology. Its exact position is unverified, so treat finding it as part of the stop.

Stop four

Elizabeth Street, the stub

126 Elizabeth St, and the Diversity Garden at Foster PlaceMap
400 m5 min

Walk north up what is left of the street. At 126 Elizabeth stood Kwong Chow, the restaurant Jean Lumb ran with her husband Doyle from 1959, the operations room of Save Chinatown. Her plaque stands in the small Diversity Garden at Elizabeth and Foster Place, a minute away. Around the corner at Dundas and Elizabeth, the Wineberg Apartments building survives from Chinatown's last phase, and the four peaked gables at 181 to 187 Dundas West are the last commercial row of it.

Stop five

Chestnut Street, and half a million objects

The courthouse at 10 Armoury St, and the site of 94 ChestnutMap
300 m4 min

In 2015, before the new courthouse went up, archaeologists dug the parking lot on this block and brought out roughly half a million artifacts: toys, shoes, musical instruments, cisterns, privies, a pre-contact Indigenous projectile point, the foundations of a Russian synagogue, and row houses built by formerly enslaved African Americans. The whole buried Ward, under a car park. The dig became the second book, The Ward Uncovered.

At 94 Chestnut stood the British Methodist Episcopal Church, bought by African-American settlers who acquired the land in 1845, drawn to Toronto for what Lorinc calls "its reputation as a hotbed of abolitionist sentiment". It offered services, schooling and meeting space to Black Toronto for over a century. It was demolished in the late 1950s after the congregation moved to Shaw Street. When Lorinc wrote in 2015, the site was a parking lot. That sentence is the bleakest single fact on this walk: the Ward's Black history was erased so completely that its church became parking.

The courthouse lobby may display dig artifacts, unverified. There were exhibit plans, but walk-in access to a working courthouse needs checking on the day. Do not count on it.
Stop six

The Poor House, and out

87 Elm StMap
300 m4 min

The House of Industry, 1848, the third founding institution: it dispensed welfare to destitute women and children, and forced men to break stones to receive their allotment. The building survives. So do a few Victorian row houses on Elm just west of Bay, possibly the last domestic fabric of the Ward, worth a look as you pass. Optional one-block detour: the 1931 Art Deco coach terminal at 610 Bay, closed since 2021, which for fifty years was where newcomers without much money actually arrived downtown.

Stop seven

Dundas West, to the Chinatown that survived

Spadina and DundasMap
1.1 km along Dundas, past the AGO15 min

This kilometre is the decompression leg, and it is also the actual route of the diaspora: the Chinese community moved west along this street through the 1950s and 60s, ahead of the wrecking ball, and rebuilt at Spadina. Find Jean Lumb Lane, the muralled laneway off Dundas between Huron and Grange Place. Her plaque stands in the Chinatown that was destroyed; her lane runs through the one she saved.

And it is being squeezed again, right now. A 2019 condo application at 315 to 325 Spadina, whose first public consultation did not mention Chinatown once, triggered a new generation of the same fight: Friends of Chinatown Toronto, a Chinatown land trust. The City's answer, the Chinatown Tomorrow plan, passed council on 30 July 2026, three weeks before your walk, and its appeal window closes on 2 September. You are walking Spadina inside the appeal window of the plan meant to decide whether this Chinatown survives its second existential squeeze. The first one was answered by Jean Lumb in 1969.

Stop eight

Anshei Minsk

10 St Andrew StMap
250 m3 min

Enter Kensington the way its Jewish community did: from Spadina. The Minsker, Byzantine Revival, completed 1930 by a congregation founded in 1912 by Jews from Minsk, was the "businessman's shul" whose service times suited the garment trade on Red Spadina, the street of needle-trade strikes. Of the forty-odd downtown congregations of the early 1930s, this and the Kiever and one other are all that remain. It is still the only Orthodox synagogue downtown with a full-time rabbi.

Stop nine

The Market, read as strata

Augusta Ave: Casa Acoreana at 235, Perola's at 247Map
300 m4 min

Kensington is the Ward's direct descendant: the Jewish families who moved here from 1905 invented its defining architecture, the shop grafted onto the front of a Victorian house, and by 1931 eighty per cent of Toronto's 45,000 Jews lived in or around these streets, with a synagogue on almost every corner. Then each layer painted over the last: Azorean Portuguese from 1953 (the bright house paint is theirs, on Jewish shopfronts, on an English estate's street grid), Caribbean and Chinese shopkeepers after 1962, Latin American refugees in the 1980s.

Perola's at 247 Augusta is the whole story in one shop: founded as a Portuguese grocery, now the Latin American anchor, confirmed open. Casa Acoreana at Baldwin and Augusta is the best surviving Portuguese-era counter. And lunch is wherever the Market takes you; nothing is scripted here because the closures are moving too fast to promise a specific counter.

Be honest about what you are walking through. Of 31 grocers trading here in 2007, 11 remained by 2025; food sellers halved while restaurants and cannabis shops multiplied. Kristin Donovan, who closed her fish shop in 2025 after 13 years: "The sad truth is that Kensington Market isn't healthy anymore." The counterweight is at 27 to 29 Kensington Ave: the community land trust bought the building in 2023 with $4 million partly raised on these streets, six affordable flats and two shops taken off the market forever. The Ward died in 1947 because it had no defenders; Kensington survived the 1969 urban renewal plan and the Spadina Expressway because it had them, and the land trust is the same fight with a new instrument.
Stop ten

The Kiever, and the pint

25 Bellevue Ave, then Ronnie's Local 069, 69 Nassau StMap
250 m, then 200 m5 min

The twin-domed Kiever Synagogue, completed 1927 by Ukrainian Jews from Kiev gubernia, stands on the exact site of the Belle Vue estate house the whole neighbourhood is named for. A congregation founded in the Ward in 1912, building on the footprint of the English gentry it replaced: the whole walk's succession argument, in one lot. "The only documented shul in Toronto known to be built by the people for the people."

Then finish at Ronnie's Local 069 on Nassau Street, the counterculture layer's clubhouse: a cash-friendly dive with the best people-watching patio in the Market. Almost certainly open, no website to verify by, which is in character.

The ending, and the two stories told without visiting

Little India, told from here. Devni asked how Toronto got its Little India, and the answer is the perfect counterexample to everything you just walked. Gerrard East's bazaar grew in the early 1970s around a single Bollywood cinema that drew South Asian audiences from across the region; shops followed the cinema, creating a Little India with no resident Indian community around it. When home video killed the cinema and the community's centre of gravity moved to Brampton and Scarborough, the strip thinned, and my earlier research confirmed the skip: it is the weakest visit on the migration theme. The lesson it teaches is the walk's closing argument: enclaves anchored only in commerce evaporate; enclaves anchored in housing leave synagogues and land trusts. Heritage Toronto's free self-guided "Big Stories in Little India" map is on their site if you ever want the ground version.

Little Jamaica is the companion trip, not a bolt-on. It is the same story on a different axis, west along Eglinton, and it is already researched and on the main page: the strip the LRT construction nearly killed, in the first year you can reach it by the train that did the damage. Do it as its own lunch outing.

And the frame to carry home comes from Doug Saunders, whose book gives this walk its name. Arrival cities, he writes, are the "Chinatowns, Little Indias, Hispanic quarters, urban slums and migrant suburbs of wealthy countries", and their paradox is this:

The crucial paradox of the arrival city is that its occupants all want to stop living in an arrival city, either by making money and moving their families and village networks out or by turning the neighborhood itself into something better.

Doug Saunders, Arrival City, 2010

The Ward's communities did the first. Kensington is trying, through a land trust, to do the second. The city demolished the one and put a plaque on the other, and the distance between those two responses is sixty years of learning, paid for by the people who lived there.

The extension: one erasure, three diasporas

If there is dinner left in you: walk Augusta north to College and west to Clinton Street, about 25 minutes, through Little Italy, which is where the Ward's Italians went, to Casa Paco at 50C Clinton, already on the main list (No. 32 in Canada's 100 Best, ~142 reviews, book ahead, Thu to Sun only). That single line on the map takes you from the erased neighbourhood through the Chinese, Jewish and Italian diasporas to dinner. Otherwise: the 510 streetcar from Spadina and College runs you back to Line 2.
